The hard part was never the app

A form builder, a contact list, a ticket queue — none of these are difficult software any more. Some people could write one in a weekend and have a half-finished one in a repository to prove it. Everybody else can now describe one and watch it get built.

What stops both of them is the same thing, and none of it is the thing anyone actually wanted. Somewhere for the information to live. A way to let the right people in and keep everyone else out. An address of its own. Copies, in case. Someone to notice when it stops working, and to know what to do about it. None of that is your business, and for most people it is not any part of their business.

So you rent the hosted version instead, and take the bill, the per-seat tax and somebody else’s roadmap along with it.

dabberz is the third option. Start from a template with the boring half already solved and proven, keep it as it is or change it by asking, and the operations stay ours. The software is yours and so is the data.

There is no settings page

Deliberately. Every tool like this eventually grows a configuration screen with four hundred checkboxes on it, because somebody had to guess in advance which things you might want to change — and whatever you actually want is never one of the four hundred.

So there isn’t one. Theming, branding, fields, wording, what happens when somebody submits the form: all of it is a request, and all of it goes through the same path below — checked, previewed, and live only when you say so. The things a settings page would have let you change are not a special category here, and the things it wouldn’t have aren’t off limits.

Asking for a change

  1. Install an app. It gets its own database, its own web address, its own copy of the code. About a minute.
  2. Say what you want changed. “Add a budget-range dropdown to the enquiry form, and make it match my brand colours.” An agent does the work on a separate machine, nowhere near your live site.
  3. Look at it. The change gets a web address of its own, so you can click around the new version yourself. Your live site hasn’t moved.
  4. It gets checked before you’re asked. The app’s own tests run, and a real browser opens the new version to confirm the pages still work — tested against a copy of your actual records, because a change tested against an empty database hasn’t been tested.
  5. You decide. Going live is a person’s decision, never the agent’s.
  6. Changed your mind? One click back to the previous version. The database is snapshotted before anything ships.

What it costs

Nothing today. Forms is free while dabberz is this early, there is no card on file, and there is no trial clock — a trial that expires once you’ve put real records into it is a trap, not an offer. You’ll get 90 days’ notice before that changes, and your data exports on the way out either way.

We would rather tell you the shape of the price before we’ve finished arguing about the number than show you a page with no number on it at all. You rent room to run things in — not seats, and not apps.

A slice, from $15 a month

Enough room for two to four apps. Run one big one or four small ones; we don’t count them and we don’t count the people using them. Spin up an experiment, decide it was a bad idea, delete it — the bill doesn’t notice.

Outgrown it? Take a bigger slice

Roughly twice the room for roughly twice the price, and so on up. Whether that is one larger machine or several smaller ones with your apps spread across them is our problem to solve, not a line on your invoice.

Storage metered above a real allowance

This is the one meter we keep, and we’d rather explain it than hide it: unbounded storage on a flat fee is a promise that gets broken quietly two years later by a company that needs the money. So there’s a generous allowance, and above it you pay close to what it costs us, visible before you cross it.

Your own Claude subscription

The agent runs on your Anthropic credentials, so the AI line on your dabberz bill is zero and cannot surprise you. If you’re the sort of person who’d have built this yourself, you almost certainly already pay for one. The thing worth knowing: an afternoon of changes draws on your own usage limits, the same allowance you use for everything else.

What we won’t do

Charge per person

Per-seat pricing taxes collaboration. Adding a colleague so they can see one dashboard shouldn’t cost a full seat, so people share logins or leave somebody out. We charge on anything that isn’t a human being.

Bill you for growing

No per-app, per-record, per-contact, per-submission or per-request pricing. Every one of those meters bills you for your own success on an axis you didn’t choose — and charging per app would punish exactly the experimenting this is for. Storage is the single exception, at cost, above an allowance: that one is physics, not a pricing strategy.

Resell you AI

We don’t mark up tokens, and agent access is included in every app rather than sold as an upgrade — the opposite of “seats, plus extra for agents”.

Make leaving hard

Every app exports everything it can import, as a database dump rather than a souvenir CSV. A platform for rescuing people from SaaS shouldn’t become the thing they were trying to leave.

Where your data goes

Step four says the agent’s work gets checked against a copy of your real records. That’s the part people ask about, so here is exactly what it means.

The copy is real, and temporary

It isn’t scrubbed or faked, because that’s the whole point of it. It is a separate database, created when the change opens and dropped when the change goes live or is thrown away.

A preview is as private as the live app

A change’s web address enforces the same sign-in rules as your installation, by the same mechanism, in the same place. Preview addresses are readable rather than secret — so it’s the rule protecting them, not the obscurity.

The agent can read those records

It has to, in order to check its own work against them. So what it reads goes to Anthropic under your subscription and your agreement with them, not ours. If that isn’t acceptable for a particular app, that app isn’t one to install here yet, and we’d rather say so than bury it.

So can we

One operator, one machine, administrator access to it. Any claim otherwise would be theatre. Encryption at rest protects the disk from someone who steals it; it does not protect you from us. What protects you is that the export works and you can leave.

Bringing your own app

This is where it’s going, and it comes with one real condition: an app has to be built against the platform’s own components — the login, the database, the storage, the mail. That contract is the entire reason we can run something without knowing what it does, change it safely and hand it back to you. An app that rolls its own authentication is not one we can host this way.

Which is the trade, stated plainly: build against the proven half, and the operations stop being your problem. Today that means starting from a template and changing it until it’s yours — the same thing from the other end, and the end that works right now. Most people will never need the other one, and nothing on this page assumes you write code.

Honest about where this is
