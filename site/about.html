About

dabberz hosts business software you own, and changes it when you ask.

The problem it exists for

Small teams pay for tools priced as though they were large ones, and the tools themselves are rarely the hard part. A form builder, a contact list, a ticket queue — these are a weekend each, and plenty of people have started one to prove it.

What stops it is never the feature anyone wanted. It is somewhere for the information to live, a way to let the right people in and keep everyone else out, an address of its own, copies in case, and somebody to notice when it stops working. Self-hosting somebody else’s open-source version swaps the bill for exactly that second job — which is a bad trade if you can do it, and not a trade at all if you can’t.

And it is the same waste every time. Everybody who builds one of these builds the same sign-in, the same list with a search box, the same way of letting somebody out again — work that has been done thousands of times and is worth nothing to your business, whether you pay for it in weekends, in a developer, or in the hours and credits of asking an AI to do it for you.

That second job is the whole product. You get software you own and data you control, and the operations are somebody else’s problem. Start from the part that is already solved, and spend what you have on the part that is actually yours.

Why there is no settings page

Software like this normally grows a configuration screen, which is a list of the changes somebody guessed in advance that you might want. It is always too long to navigate and never contains the thing you came for, and the moment you want something off the list you are filing a feature request or hiring someone.

dabberz has no such screen. Branding, colours, wording, fields, what happens when a form is submitted — these are not settings, they are changes to the app, and they go through the route below like any other change. The effect is that there is no boundary between what you are allowed to configure and what would require a developer, because the same mechanism handles both.

How a change actually works

When you ask for something to be different, nothing touches your live site. The change gets a machine of its own, a separate copy of the code, a web address of its own, and its own copy of your data — taken a moment before the work starts.

That last part matters more than it sounds. A change tested against an empty database has not been tested. The question you actually have is whether this still works with your records in it, and the only way to answer that is to try it against a copy of them.

Then it is checked twice: the app’s own tests, which know what the important paths are, and a real browser opening the preview to confirm the page loads and renders. If either fails, the change is blocked. If both pass, you are told what was checked — and what wasn’t, because an automated check can confirm the page works and cannot confirm it does what you meant.

Promotion is always a person’s decision. The agent proposes; it never ships.

The copy of your data is the part people ask about, so: it is a real copy rather than a scrubbed or invented one, it lives in a separate database created when the change opens and dropped when the change ships or is thrown away, and the change’s web address enforces the same sign-in rules as your live installation — by the same mechanism, in the same place. Preview addresses are readable rather than secret, so it is that rule protecting them and not the obscurity. The agent can read those records, because checking its own work against them is the point; what it reads goes to whoever provides your AI, under your own agreement with them rather than ours.

Agents, and who pays for them

Software increasingly charges for seats and then charges again for the AI features on those seats. We think that is backwards, so asking for changes is part of every app on the platform, and you bring your own AI agent rather than buying it from us at a markup. Today the platform drives Claude Code specifically; what that costs you, and who you buy it from, is not our business and not our margin.

It also means we aren’t reselling somebody else’s margin, which keeps the incentives straight: we make money by running things well, not by you using more of something we buy wholesale.

The honest cost of that arrangement, which is easy to leave out of a sentence like the one above: it is a separate subscription if you don’t already have one, and a long session of changes draws on your own usage limits — the same allowance you use for your own work.

What’s yours, and what’s ours

Worth separating, because “you own it” can be made to cover a lot. The app is yours: its code, its database, and an export you can take whenever you like without asking us. The platform — the routing, the identity layer, the preview machinery, the backups — is what you are paying us to operate.

That split has a consequence we would rather state than have you discover. Apps here never implement their own login, because the platform tells them who is asking. That is what makes them small and consistent, and it means an app lifted out of dabberz needs something to take over that job before it can let anyone in. The interface it expects is deliberately a boring one, and it is documented with the rest of the platform rather than left for you to reverse engineer.

That same boundary is what will let you bring an app of your own. The condition is that it has to be built against the platform’s components — identity, database, storage, mail — because that contract is precisely what lets us operate something without knowing what it does. An app that implements its own authentication is not one we can host this way. Today the practical route is the other end of the same deal: start from an app in the catalog and change it until it is yours.

Apps dabberz publishes are AGPL-3.0, declared in each app’s manifest. The platform itself is headed for MIT — the point of this is that you can leave with the thing you were running, and a restrictive licence on the half that runs it would make that a slogan rather than an exit.

Who builds it

dabberz is built and operated by Dab Media LLC, a small US company that also runs Kompare, Kollectibles and Gamer Galaxy.

The name is older than this version of it. dabberz began as game servers and shared web hosting — renting people a reliable piece of somebody else’s machine so they didn’t have to run their own. This is the same business with a different lever: the thing being hosted can now be changed by asking, which is what makes it worth owning rather than renting.

The platform is developed in the open. The source, the design documents, and an honest list of what is unfinished are all on GitHub — including the parts that are not done yet, because a list of known gaps is more useful to you than a list of features.

Where it is today

Early. One app in the catalog, one machine, one operator. More apps are planned — the point of the platform is a suite that shares the same database, identity, mail and storage underneath, so your CRM and your help desk are not two separate products with two separate bills.
